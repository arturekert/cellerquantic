In vino veritas

Wine, science and other truths

Physicists like to know how things work, wine included. Here are some of our favourite stories where the two meet — trivia for the table, and a few things worth knowing before the next glass.

  • Physics

    The constant on our label never ends

    Every bottle carries , the reduced Planck constant, ħ = h/2π. Since 2019 the Planck constant h has been fixed exactly, at 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, by the definition of the kilogram. Dividing by 2π makes ħ irrational: its digits never end and never repeat. Our label prints the first 121 — far more than any experiment needs, and infinitely fewer than a mathematician would like.

  • Physics

    The tears of wine

    Swirl a glass and drops form on the inside and run back down. Alcohol evaporates faster than water from the thin film near the rim, which raises the surface tension there and pulls more wine up the glass until it gathers and falls. James Thomson, Lord Kelvin’s brother, explained it in 1855; Carlo Marangoni studied it a decade later, and it now bears his name. The tears say a lot about alcohol and very little about quality.

  • Chemistry

    Pasteur and the mirror-image molecules

    In 1848 a young Louis Pasteur looked closely at crystals of a salt of racemic acid, a by-product of winemaking. He noticed two kinds, mirror images of one another, and separated them by hand with tweezers. Dissolved, one turned polarised light to the left and the other to the right. It was the discovery of molecular chirality — the handedness of molecules, which matters today in everything from biology to medicines.

  • Biology

    Who does the fermenting?

    Pasteur went on to show, in the 1850s and 1860s, that fermentation is the work of living yeast. In 1897 Eduard Buchner went one step further: he fermented sugar with a juice pressed from yeast containing no living cells at all. The work was being done by enzymes. That experiment is often called the birth of biochemistry, and it earned Buchner the 1907 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

  • History

    Pasteurisation began with wine, not milk

    In his Études sur le vin (1866) Pasteur proposed warming wine gently, to around 50–60 °C, to kill the microbes that turned it sour, without spoiling its taste. The method was later applied to milk and to almost everything else we eat and drink, and took his name.

  • Nuclear physics

    Bottles that remember the atomic age

    Atmospheric nuclear tests from 1945 onwards spread traces of caesium-137 around the globe, and wines made since then carry a tiny amount. In Bordeaux, the nuclear physicist Philippe Hubert developed a way to measure it through the glass, without opening the bottle — a wine claiming to be older than 1945 should show none. Radioactivity and wine go back further still: in 1954 Willard Libby, of radiocarbon-dating fame, used vintage wines to study natural tritium.

  • Archaeology

    Eight thousand years of wine

    Chemical traces of grape wine in pottery jars from two Neolithic villages in Georgia, Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora, date to around 6000 BC — the oldest firm evidence of winemaking yet found (McGovern and colleagues, PNAS, 2017). Our 18th‑century cellar in Gaianes is, by comparison, rather young.

  • Botany

    The insect that nearly ended European wine

    In the 1860s a tiny aphid from North America, phylloxera, reached France and began killing vines by attacking their roots. Within a few decades it had destroyed most of Europe’s vineyards. The cure was ecological rather than chemical: grafting European vines onto the roots of resistant American species. To this day almost every vine in Europe, ours included, grows on American roots.

  • Physics

    A million bubbles

    In sparkling wine, bubbles do not form on imperfections in the glass but on microscopic cellulose fibres left from drying cloths or floating in the air, which trap tiny pockets of gas. The physicist Gérard Liger-Belair in Reims, who has spent a career filming them, estimates that a flute of champagne releases around a million bubbles.

  • Chemistry

    A nose that counts in parts per trillion

    “Corked” wine is usually caused by 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, or TCA. Many people can smell it at a few nanograms per litre — the equivalent of a few milligrams dissolved in an Olympic swimming pool. Few laboratory instruments are as sensitive as a trained nose.

  • Our namesakes

    Lise Meitner

    Our white is named after the Austrian physicist who, with her nephew Otto Frisch, gave the first physical explanation of nuclear fission in 1939. The Nobel Prize for the discovery went to Otto Hahn alone. Element 109, meitnerium, is named after her — and now, more modestly, a Garnacha Blanca from Gaianes.

  • Our namesakes

    Max Born

    Our red honours the physicist who realised that the wave function in quantum mechanics describes probabilities: the square of its amplitude gives the chance of finding a particle in a given place. It is known as the Born rule, and it earned him the 1954 Nobel Prize in Physics. Each bottle, like each measurement, keeps its outcome until it is opened.

  • Words

    In vino veritas

    “In wine, truth.” The Latin saying echoes the Greek poet Alcaeus, who wrote of “wine and truth” in the 6th century BC; Pliny the Elder noted that truth was commonly ascribed to wine, and Erasmus made the phrase famous in his collection of proverbs. Scientists, who spend their lives looking for truth, have always found it a congenial place to look.

“Wine is sunlight, held together by water.”Attributed to Galileo Galilei — probably apocryphally, but we like it
